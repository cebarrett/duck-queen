# Backstory: The Diminished Crown

## The Old Queendom

There was a time when every pond between the willow banks and the windgrass flats
answered to a duck in a crown.

Not every duck knew this, of course. Most ducks knew only the shining surface of
the water, the sudden joy of bread, the panic of a falling leaf, and the ancient
duck conviction that anything small enough to swallow was probably food. But above
that bright, silly, vulnerable life there rose, generation after generation, a
lineage of unusual hens: white-feathered, clear-eyed, long-memoried, and burdened
with the strange intelligence of queens.

They were not gods. They were ducks. They loved mud, warm stones, and the
drama of a good quack. Yet they could count seasons, remember borders, plan for
famine, recognize ambition in a goose's eye, and teach a flock to gather before a
storm. That was the miracle of the Duck Queendom. One mind, crowned in gold,
trying to shepherd a civilization whose citizens could be distracted from matters
of state by a beetle.

For a while, impossibly, it worked.

The Queendom was never an empire in the human sense. It had no walls and few
written laws. It was a pattern laid over water and grass: reed banners on hummocks,
safe nesting mounds, remembered food patches, warning calls passed from pond to
pond, and the royal right of rally. A Queen quacked, and ducks came. Not because
they understood policy, but because some deep feathered part of them knew that the
Queen's call meant shelter, food, and the old promise that no duckling would be
left behind if the Crown could help it.

The geese understood more than the ducks did.

They watched the Queendom from the marshes: tall, grey, cold-eyed birds with
voices like cracked horns and a gift for standing where they were least wanted.
Geese admired order, but not mercy. They admired strength, but not care. Where
ducks forgot insults by sunset, geese remembered a slight for years. Where ducks
argued by quacking until everyone became confused and went swimming, geese formed
lines, held territory, and advanced one muddy foot at a time.

The first Queens beat them anyway.

The later Queens merely slowed them.

By the time the current Queen breaks her shell into history, the Queendom has
shrunken to one pond, a few ducklings, some half-rotten reeds, and a crown whose
gold is brighter than its authority. The geese hold the marsh. The old banners are
gone or buried. The great nests have fallen silent. The ducks remember almost
nothing.

But ruins remember.

So do crowns.

## The Seven Crowns

The current monarch is usually called the Seventh Queen of the Duck Queendom.
Some old nests count her as the Sixth. The difference matters.

One Queen was erased.

The protagonist does not know this at the beginning. She knows only that she is a
Queen because the crown fits her head, because ducklings follow when she calls,
and because somewhere beyond the pond a goose is laughing.

Her journey across the map becomes a journey backward through her own bloodline.
Each reclaimed biome reveals an older layer of the Queendom: what a previous Queen
built, what she feared, what she misunderstood, and what the geese learned from
defeating her.

The tragedy of the dynasty is not that the Queens were foolish. They were mostly
competent. Many were kind. A few were brilliant. They lost anyway, because their
kingdom was made of ducks, and ducks are difficult material from which to build a
state.

That is the fantasy at the heart of the game: not power fantasy alone, but
leadership fantasy. The Queen is not trying to command perfect soldiers. She is
trying to care for hungry, waddling, distractible, lovable subjects long enough to
make history turn around.

## Queen I: Hespera First-Feather

Before Hespera, there were ponds but no Queendom.

Ducks nested where they could. Ducklings vanished to foxes, floods, snapping
turtles, cold nights, and the simple disaster of wandering away because something
sparkled in the reeds. The geese already held the deeper marsh, but in those days
they were content to bully at the edges. There was little worth ruling. A duck who
found food ate it. A duck who heard danger fled alone. A duck who remembered a safe
path might forget it when the sun came out.

Hespera remembered everything.

She was hatched in a spring flood, swept from her nest, and carried through three
ponds before she was old enough to stand. When she returned, she knew the shape of
the land better than any duck alive. She knew which stones stayed dry, which reeds
hid water, where foxes crossed, and where ducklings could climb from a flooded
bank. She began to quack before storms. Ducks came because she was loud. They
survived because she was right.

Her crown was not gold at first. It was a ring of yellow reeds twisted by
ducklings who liked the way it bobbed on her head. The first royal banner was not
a symbol of conquest. It was a reed stuck in a mound to mark dry ground.

Hespera's gift was gathering. She taught the first law of the Queendom:

> A duck alone is lunch. A flock is weather.

Under her, the First Pond became safe. Then the Second Pond. Then the low
meadow pools where ducklings could feed without being taken. Hespera never called
herself a monarch. Others did, in the loose and quacky way ducks name things. By
the end of her life, every safe mound had a reed marker, every marker carried her
scent, and every duck knew that the white hen's call meant come now.

The geese tested her only once.

A young gander strode into the First Pond and honked until half the flock hid
under lilies. Hespera climbed a stone and quacked back. Not louder. Longer. She
called and called until every duck in the meadow answered. The sound rose from
water, grass, mud, and sky. The gander retreated, not because he was afraid of
Hespera, but because he was afraid of what answered her.

That was the first honk-off. The geese did not forget it.

Hespera's artifact is the Reed Ring, dry and brittle but still curved to the
shape of a crown. It teaches the player that rulership begins with rallying, not
defeating.

## Queen II: Bellwade the Patient

Hespera founded the Queendom. Bellwade made it work.

She inherited a miracle and immediately saw its flaws. Ducks gathered when called,
but they did not stay gathered. They ate through stores, nested in foolish places,
forgot safe routes, and treated every pond as if it were the only pond in the
world. Hespera had saved them by instinct and presence. Bellwade understood that
presence would not be enough.

She became the first Queen to plan for ducks who would never understand the plan.

Bellwade built the Nest Ledger, not a book in ink but a chain of places: marked
mounds, tucked feathers, stones arranged by beak, paths trampled through reeds,
and little caches of food hidden above the floodline. Each pond had a rhythm. Each
flock had a keeper. No keeper was especially wise, but each could be taught one
important thing. This duck watches the east reeds. That duck leads ducklings to
the warm bank. That duck is not allowed near the food cache unless supervised.

The Queendom grew because Bellwade accepted the ridiculousness of her people.
She did not try to make ducks less ducklike. She built systems that survived
ducklikeness.

The geese responded with patience of their own.

They stopped challenging the Queen directly. Instead, they learned theft. A goose
would stand near a cache until the ducks wandered off. A goose would occupy a
narrow bank and make the flock take the long way around. A goose would honk near a
nursery, not to attack, just to scatter everyone at the worst possible time. The
geese discovered that the Queendom's strength was gathering, so they made
gathering expensive.

Bellwade beat them through logistics. She multiplied caches, moved nurseries,
and created the first royal food law: no pond was full until the next pond was
fed. It was a humane law, and a difficult one. Ducks prefer visible food. Future
generations would quietly resent it.

Her reign was long, prosperous, and full of tired triumph. She died old, respected,
and surrounded by ducklings who had no idea why their world was so safe.

Her artifact is the Nest Bell, a hollow reed charm that once called keeper-ducks
to their posts. It teaches that a large flock needs support, not merely numbers.

## Queen III: Malloria Many-Brooded

Malloria was beloved before she was wise.

She was beautiful, loud, generous, and nearly impossible to ignore. Ducks followed
her for the pleasure of following. Where Bellwade had built systems, Malloria
built belonging. She welcomed every bird who would answer the royal call: mallards
from the meadow pools, white farm ducks escaped from distant yards, wood ducks
from shaded water, old drakes with bad tempers, ducklings with no surviving brood,
even a few strange migratory cousins who stayed for one season and left the next
with royal ribbons tied to their memories.

Under Malloria, the Queendom became magnificent.

It also became noisy beyond reason.

Her great invention was the Chorus. A single Queen could outlast one goose. A
flock could drown a marsh. Malloria learned to layer calls: ducklings peeping high,
mallards rasping low, white ducks carrying the middle, the Queen cutting through
all of it with a command that could turn panic into music. The geese hated the
Chorus. It made their honks sound lonely.

Malloria drove the geese out of the Reed Marsh for the first time. She raised
pink banners on their old honk posts. She held festivals in the mud. She declared
that every duck, from proud drake to baffled duckling, was a subject of equal
dignity beneath the Crown.

It was noble. It was sincere. It was hard to govern.

Different ducks wanted different things. Meadow ducks wanted grain and sun.
Marsh ducks wanted cover. Wood ducks wanted trees. Ducklings wanted whatever they
had just seen another duckling eat. The more Malloria loved her subjects, the more
she promised them. The more she promised them, the more the Queendom depended on
her ability to be everywhere.

The geese learned a cruel lesson from her reign: a diverse flock was powerful
when united, but fragile when tired. They began to honk at the edges, never enough
to force a final battle, always enough to pull the Chorus out of tune. They spread
rumors in the language of territory: this pond is unsafe, that banner is weak, the
Queen cannot hear you from so far away.

Malloria never truly lost a honk-off. She lost distance.

By the end of her reign, the Queendom was larger than any one call could cross.
She died with her head under her wing, listening to three ponds quack for her and
four ponds remain silent because they had not heard the news.

Her artifact is the Many-Brood Feather, bright with colors from every flock she
welcomed. It teaches that different duck types should matter, but unity must be
maintained.

## Queen IV: Fenna Reedwise

Fenna inherited Malloria's splendor and Bellwade's worries.

She was not beloved at first. She was narrow-eyed, mud-footed, and practical. She
did not enjoy festivals. She inspected drainage. She bit through decorative
ribbons if they tangled a duckling's foot. She believed that the Queendom would
survive only if it became less dependent on the Queen's physical presence.

Fenna built paths.

Reed bridges, lily routes, mud causeways, hidden channels through cattails,
stepping stones marked with pale feathers: she made the land itself remember the
Queendom. A frightened flock could follow a banner to a mound, a mound to a food
cache, a cache to a safe waterway. Her Queendom was not as glorious as Malloria's,
but it was harder to break.

Then she made her mistake.

She negotiated with the geese.

Fenna was intelligent enough to recognize that the geese were not monsters. They
had broods too. They wanted nesting ground, food access, and prestige. They were
aggressive, proud, and territorial, but not mindless. Fenna believed a border
could hold if both sides profited from it.

For nine seasons, it did.

The Treaty Stone stood at the edge of the Reed Marsh. Ducks nested south of it.
Geese nested north. Shared water remained shared. Honk-offs were ceremonial,
fought at marked posts and settled without pursuit. Fenna's reign became the most
peaceful in the dynasty's memory.

Peace changed the ducks faster than it changed the geese.

Ducks began to treat banners as decorations. Keeper-ducks abandoned posts that
had not seen danger in years. Food laws softened. Young subjects grew up thinking
the geese were unpleasant neighbors rather than rivals with a long plan. The geese,
meanwhile, taught every gosling where the borders were and why they would one day
move.

When Fenna grew old, the geese did not break the treaty. They interpreted it.
They stood one foot over the line. They occupied shared water for "temporary
nesting." They honked during duck festivals and called it ceremony. Each challenge
was too small to justify war and too deliberate to ignore.

Fenna spent her last years defending a peace that had become a trap.

Her artifact is the Broken Treaty Stone, half-sunk in mud. It teaches that mercy
without vigilance becomes an invitation.

## Queen V: Plumerelle of the Standards

Plumerelle was born under banners and trusted symbols too much.

She came to the crown during a crisis of confidence. The geese had not conquered
the Queendom, but they had made it uncertain. Ducks avoided border ponds. Some
flocks stopped answering distant rally calls. The old routes still existed, yet
fewer subjects trusted them. Plumerelle understood that the Queendom needed to be
seen.

She became the Queen of Standards.

Her banners were everywhere: reed-poles crowned with white feathers, little flags
painted with berry juice, royal markers on stones, mound crests, nest signs, pond
totems, floating lilies tied in gold grass. A duck could waddle almost anywhere
and see evidence that the Crown had not abandoned her.

It worked, beautifully and dangerously.

Flocks returned to old ponds. Ducklings learned the banner shapes before they
learned the safe paths beneath them. Honk-offs became ceremonies again, with the
Queen arriving beneath a fan of flags and the Chorus rising behind her. The geese
retreated from several banks simply because the ducks looked numerous and
organized.

But symbols can feed pride faster than they feed ducklings.

Plumerelle's keepers spent more time maintaining banners than checking caches.
Some ponds displayed royal colors while their nests thinned. Ducks rallied to
standards and then wondered what they were meant to do there. Plumerelle, who was
good and brave, mistook visibility for health. She saw flags and thought the
Queendom had returned.

The geese learned to attack meaning.

They knocked down banners at night. They dragged royal markers into mud. They
stood beside broken standards and honked until ducklings associated the symbols of
safety with fear. They did not need to destroy every banner. They only needed to
make ducks doubt what banners meant.

Plumerelle fought back by raising more.

By the end, the Queendom was bright with flags and hollow in places a Queen should
have felt. When a hard winter came, several outer ponds had beautiful standards
and insufficient food. Ducks survived, but they stopped believing that royal signs
guaranteed royal care.

Her artifact is the Faded Standard. It teaches that morale is not decoration. A
banner must do something.

## Queen VI: Stormbill the Necessary

Stormbill was the Queen everyone later blamed because she was the one who looked
most like a conqueror.

She did not begin cruel. She began frightened.

She hatched in the hard winter after Plumerelle's decline, when hungry ducks
pecked at frozen mud and geese stood fat in the north marsh. She grew up hearing
that the old Queens had been too trusting, too generous, too fond of ceremony.
Stormbill concluded that the geese understood only force of voice, so voice she
would give them.

She strengthened the Chorus into a weapon of morale. She trained ducks to answer
in waves. She held night rallies. She reclaimed food caches, broke goose patrols,
and drove three ganders from the Windgrass Flats in a single season. For the first
time since Malloria, the geese looked uncertain.

Stormbill's great honk-offs became legend. She would stand on a mound in rain,
crown black with water, wings half-spread, and quack until the flock became a
storm around her. Geese fled. Ducks cheered. Lost ponds returned.

She saved the Queendom from immediate collapse.

She also taught it to depend on emergency.

Every problem became a rally. Every border became a front. Every goose became an
enemy to drive away. Stormbill recovered territory faster than Bellwade's systems
could support it, faster than Fenna's routes could be restored, faster than
Plumerelle's banners could regain trust. Her courage outran her infrastructure.

The geese adapted. They stopped meeting her where the Chorus was strong. They
harried the weak places, scattered ducklings, stole from reclaimed caches, and
retreated before the Queen arrived. Stormbill could win any battle she reached.
She could not reach all of them.

Her final campaign ended at the Black Reed Marsh, the old heart of goose power.
The songs say she broke the Marsh Baron's voice. The nests say she returned alone.
After that, Stormbill forbade several records, abandoned several exposed ponds,
and ordered the Crown to preserve the inner Queendom at any cost.

This is why some histories count the current Queen as the Sixth.

Stormbill erased her successor before that successor had a chance to reign.

Her artifact is the Broken War Standard. It teaches power, but also the danger of
winning faster than the flock can heal.

## Queen VI-and-a-Half: The Missing Queen

Her name was Luma.

Most ducks forgot her. Some were encouraged to. Some never knew she existed. In
the old nests she appears as a gap: a pressed feather with no label, a crown mark
scratched out, a clutch record with one line bitten through.

Luma was Stormbill's daughter, though not much like her. Where Stormbill was
thunder, Luma was listening. She inherited a Queendom that had survived by
shrinking. The inner ponds were safer, but poorer. The outer flocks felt
abandoned. The geese held the marshes again, and this time they held them with
stories: the Crown leaves, the Crown forgets, the Crown returns only to demand
your voice.

Luma believed the Queendom had failed not because previous Queens were weak, but
because each had solved one problem so completely that her solution became the
next Queen's burden.

Hespera gathered, so the Queendom depended on gathering.
Bellwade organized, so the Queendom depended on keepers.
Malloria welcomed, so the Queendom grew beyond one voice.
Fenna made peace, so vigilance softened.
Plumerelle raised symbols, so symbols replaced care.
Stormbill fought, so emergency became normal.

Luma wanted a Queendom that could survive without a perfect Queen.

It was the wisest ambition any duck monarch had ever held, and the hardest to
explain to ducks.

She tried to teach ponds to govern themselves. She asked mallards to hold their
own chorus, ducklings to follow local brood-keepers, old drakes to remember
routes, white ducks to maintain banners, wood ducks to watch tree nests, and all
of them to share food even when the Queen was not there to insist. Some did. Many
tried. Several forgot halfway through and went swimming.

The geese recognized the threat.

A Queendom dependent on one Queen could be outmaneuvered. A Queendom that taught
ducks to care for one another might endure. So the geese did what they had learned
to do over generations: they attacked trust. They did not merely steal food. They
stole credit. They drove ducks from caches and let other ducks be blamed. They
stood beside fallen banners and honked in Luma's cadence. They made the Queen's
own call uncertain.

Then came the Silent Molt.

No duck agrees on what happened. Some say Luma entered the Black Reed Marsh to
negotiate with the Gander General. Some say she found an old goose treaty older
than Hespera. Some say Stormbill, ashamed of her daughter's softness, refused to
send the Chorus after her. Some say Luma was not killed at all, but chose exile
rather than become another Queen whose answer would harden into doctrine.

The only certain fact is that Luma did not return.

After her disappearance, Stormbill's surviving keepers erased her from the
lineage. Not from malice alone. They feared what her failure would do to morale.
If the kindest Queen had vanished trying to trust the flock, what hope remained?
Better to call her an unhatched reign. Better to count around her. Better to
pretend the dynasty had bent, not broken.

The geese remembered her name.

They still honk it in the marsh when the mist is low.

Her artifact is the Silent Crown Shard, a piece of gold without a setting. It
teaches the final lesson: a Queendom cannot be restored by repeating the past. It
must become something the past never managed to be.

## The Current Queen

The protagonist begins with almost nothing: one pond, a handful of ducklings, a
crown too large for the state beneath it, and an inheritance made mostly of
absence.

She is not the strongest Queen. Not at first. She is young, often ridiculous, and
surrounded by subjects who will follow her into destiny unless they see a snack
first. Her first victories are small: gather a duckling, find food, reclaim a reed
patch, win a honk-off against a goose who expected her to run.

But she has one advantage her ancestors did not.

She can learn from all of them.

From Hespera, she learns to rally.
From Bellwade, she learns to prepare.
From Malloria, she learns to welcome difference.
From Fenna, she learns that routes and borders matter.
From Plumerelle, she learns that symbols must shelter what they signify.
From Stormbill, she learns that courage can save and exhaust a kingdom.
From Luma, she learns that the Queendom must be more than the Queen.

As she explores, the map becomes a history of failure and a set of tools for
repair. Old nests unlock memory echoes. Broken banners become rally points. Treaty
stones reveal contested borders. Food caches teach logistics. Lost duck types
bring forgotten strengths back into the Chorus. Every boss goose is not just an
enemy, but a keeper of some wound the dynasty never healed.

The endgame is not simply taking the marsh.

It is standing where the geese ended the old Queendom and calling every kind of
duck back into one living, chaotic, stubborn answer.

If she wins, she does not restore the old empire exactly. That empire failed.

She founds the New Queendom: sillier, humbler, better fed, harder to scatter, and
wise enough to know that even the stupidest duck is still a subject of the Crown.

## The Geese

The geese are not evil in the simple way. They are worse for a fantasy story:
they are coherent.

They remember. They organize. They teach their young. They understand territory
as inheritance and pressure as language. A goose does not need to hate ducks to
push them from a bank. He needs only to believe, with perfect sincerity, that any
place he can hold was meant to be held by geese.

Their civilization is smaller than the Duck Queendom was, but more disciplined.
They have no patience for duck chaos, duck mercy, or duck ceremony. To a goose,
the Crown is absurd: a shiny object on a bird trying to make fools behave like a
people. Yet the geese have always feared the moment when the absurd works. When
ducks answer together, the marsh changes.

Each boss goose carries a lesson learned from defeating a Queen:

- The Bragging Goose mocks Hespera's old rally and tries to make the first flock
  scatter.
- The Cache-Thief Gander exploits Bellwade's food laws and starves morale.
- The Marsh Baron breaks Malloria's Chorus by splitting duck types apart.
- The Treaty Goose twists Fenna's borders and punishes naive trust.
- The Standard-Breaker destroys Plumerelle's banners and makes symbols unsafe.
- The Storm Gander refuses Stormbill's grand confrontations and attacks the
  flock's weak edges.
- The Gander General remembers Luma, fears her lesson, and understands that the
  current Queen is dangerous only if her subjects learn to remain a Queendom when
  she is not directly beside them.

The final conflict should feel ancient, comic, and serious all at once: one
crowned duck trying to prove that care can be a form of power, opposed by geese
who have spent generations proving that pressure is easier.

## Story Tone

Duck Queen should read as cozy fantasy with real melancholy underneath.

The surface is funny: waddling monarchs, overdramatic honk-offs, ducklings who
forget matters of succession because mud exists. The deeper story is about the
burden of inheritance, the limits of benevolent rule, and the painful fact that
good intentions do not preserve a kingdom unless they become practices ordinary
subjects can carry.

The old Queens should not be treated as fools. Each was right about something.
Each was wrong about what her rightness would cost later. Their artifacts should
feel precious because they are not merely upgrades. They are apologies, warnings,
and unfinished work.

The current Queen's triumph is not that she is smarter than every ancestor.

Her triumph is that she listens to them, forgives them, refuses to worship them,
and builds something that can outlive her.
