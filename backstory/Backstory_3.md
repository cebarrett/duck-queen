# The Least of the Crown

### *A history of one night in the declining Queendom, after the manner of Victor Hugo*

---

> *To love is to act. And there is no act so small, performed in the dark, for one
> who cannot repay it and will not remember it, that it does not enlarge the world.*

---

## I. A Word Upon Crowns

Let the reader consider what a crown is.

It is, to begin with, a very little thing. A ring of gold, or of reed, or of
twisted yellow grass; a circle that fits a head and signifies that the head
beneath it is to be obeyed. Men have died in heaps for such circles. Ducks, who
are wiser than men in this one particular, have mostly died for bread, and warmth,
and the sudden inexplicable conviction that a falling leaf is the end of all
things. But the ducks too had their crown, and their crown too was a little thing,
and the history of the Duck Queendom is the history of a great error made about a
little thing.

The error was this. They believed the crown was the Queendom.

It is an easy error. It is, perhaps, the easiest error in the world, and the most
forgivable, and the most fatal, and it is made not by fools but by the wise, for
only the wise are tempted by it. A crown is visible. A crown gathers. A crown
quacks upon a stone in the rain and ten thousand frightened birds remember at once
that they are not alone, and the remembering saves them, and the one who made them
remember comes in time to believe that she is the remembering itself — that without
her white feather upon the water the whole bright silly civilization would forget
it had ever been a civilization and dissolve again into a scatter of hungry
individuals looking, each of them, at a beetle.

And she is right. That is the terrible thing. For seven generations she was right.

But a truth that is true only so long as one creature is awake is not a foundation.
It is a vigil. And no one, not the bravest hen that ever stood crowned against the
grey advancing geese, can keep a vigil forever; for the body tires, and the voice
cracks, and the night is long, and even Queens must at the last put their heads
beneath their wings. The Queendom that depends upon a Queen has written, into the
very article of its strength, the precise hour of its undoing. It has only to wait
for her to sleep.

The geese understood this. We shall come to the geese. They are not what you think.

## II. The Pond That Was Left

By the season of which I write — a cold and watchful season, when the mist lay upon
the low water from the going-down of the sun until well after the herons had risen
— the Queendom that had once laid its gentle pattern over every pond between the
willow banks and the windgrass flats had shrunk to a single pond, and the pond was
not a good one.

It was shallow at the south end, where the ducklings could feed, and that was the
mercy of it. It was choked at the north end with the black reeds that the geese
loved, and that was the threat of it. Between mercy and threat lay perhaps a
hundred wingspans of open water, and a few rotting hummocks crowned with the grey
stumps of banners that no living duck could have told you the meaning of, and a
crooked stone, half-sunk, that the oldest birds avoided without remembering why.
(It had been a Treaty Stone. A Queen named Fenna had believed in it. But that is
another history, and a sadder one, and the ducks had done with it what ducks do
with history, which is to forget it and waddle over it on the way to lunch.)

Upon this pond lived something between a flock and a memory of a flock.

There were forty ducks, or thereabouts; the number changed, for ducks are not a
quantity that holds still to be counted. There were the mallards, who wanted grain
and sun and the right to complain about both. There were two white farm ducks, fat
and anxious, descended from birds who had escaped some distant yard and never
quite escaped the yard in their hearts. There was a single wood duck, beautiful and
useless and aware of both, who roosted in the one good tree and came down only to
be admired. There were the old drakes, bad-tempered to a bird, who had survived by
being too sour to swallow. And there were the ducklings — always the ducklings —
peeping, paddling, drowning very nearly and being hauled out by the scruff,
forgetting within the hour the catastrophe they had just survived, and going at
once to investigate the next one.

And there was the Queen.

She was young. I will not give you her name, for her name is not the point, and to
make her name the point would be to make again the very error this history exists
to mourn. She was young, and the crown was too large for her, and it slipped, and
she had a way of jerking her head to keep it on that robbed her of all majesty at
precisely the moments she most required it. She was, to be plain, a little
ridiculous. The wood duck thought so. The white ducks thought so. The geese, when
they spoke of her at all, said *she is loud,* which from a goose is the whole of
contempt.

She was also the only thing standing between forty ducks and the patient grey
water of the north, and she knew it, and the knowing was eating her alive.

## III. Hodge

I must now tell you of Hodge, because this is his history, though he never knew it
was, and would not have understood it if you had told him, and would have gone, on
being told, to look at something in the mud.

Hodge was an old drake, and he was, by the common and unanimous and entirely
correct judgment of the pond, the stupidest duck in the Queendom.

Let us be just to him, since no one else ever was. He was not stupid in the way of
a duckling, which is the stupidity of having only just arrived in the world and
not yet learned its furniture. He was stupid in the deeper, more settled, more
respectable way of a creature who has been in the world a long time and learned
exactly one thing from it and stopped. He was half-blind in the left eye. His feet
were splayed. He had a temper that arrived before his thought and a thought, when
it eventually arrived, that was usually wrong. He had outlived three mates and an
indeterminate number of broods and remembered none of them with any precision,
which the other ducks took for callousness and which was in fact only fog.

He held a post. This is the single dignity of his life and we must give it its
weight. At the east edge of the pond, where a finger of reed ran out toward the
windgrass, Hodge stood watch. He had stood watch there for as long as anyone could
recall, and longer than anyone could recall why. Some Queen, generations gone, in
the great vanished days when the Crown had a keeper-duck for every reed and a law
for every keeper, had given some ancestor of Hodge's the east reeds to watch; and
the law had died, and the Queen had died, and the reason had died, and the empire
that needed watching had shrunk to a bad pond a child could cross — and still, each
dusk, the old fool waddled to the east edge and stood, facing the windgrass,
watching for an enemy that the others had long since stopped believing would come
from that direction.

They laughed at him for it. Gently, mostly; ducks are not cruel, only forgetful,
which on certain nights amounts to the same thing. *There goes Hodge to guard the
grass,* they said. *Hodge is keeping the windgrass from invading.* And Hodge, who
heard them, and who was too stupid to be wounded or too wise to show it — even now
I cannot tell you which, and I have thought about Hodge a great deal — Hodge said
nothing, and went, and stood, and watched the empty land go dark.

O reader, do not laugh at Hodge. I am about to ask you to love him, and I will not
have you laughing at him when the time comes.

## IV. The Geese, Who Were Not Cruel

Across the cold water, in the black reeds at the north end, the geese were not
sleeping.

I have promised you that the geese are not what you think, and I must keep the
promise now, before the night begins, because if you go into this night believing
the geese are wicked you will draw from it the wrong lesson, and the wrong lesson
from this night has killed more ducks than ever the geese did.

The geese were not wicked. This is the hardest thing in the whole history to
believe, and the ducks could never afford to believe it, and the not-believing was
the geese's chief and patient weapon. The ducks required their enemy to be cruel,
because a cruel enemy can be hated, and hatred is warm, and a flock that hates
together is a flock that has, for one splendid furious hour, forgotten that it is
cold and hungry and afraid. The geese gave the ducks no such warmth. They were not
cruel. They were *consistent,* which is far worse, and far harder to honk at, and
against which no chorus has ever in seven generations prevailed.

A goose did not stand in the black reeds dreaming of dead ducklings. He stood there
because the reeds, in his settled and sincere and unhateful opinion, had always
been meant for geese; and any duckling presently in them was a small error in the
arrangement of the world, which he, as a goose of conscience, was obliged in the
fullness of time to correct. He felt about the duckling roughly what you feel about
a weed in good soil. Not hatred. Tidiness.

And so they did not attack that night, not in the way the songs mean by attack.
They had learned, across the burying of six crowns, that you do not break a
Queendom by storming it; you break it by *waiting,* and by moving one foot at a
time forever, and by being awake the precise instant the Crown falls asleep. They
stood in the reeds, grey and coherent and unhurried, and they watched the young
Queen's white feather move and move and move upon the black water, tending, soothing,
gathering, answering — and they waited, with the whole of their cold and patient
hearts, for the feather to stop.

For a duck, even a crowned one, must at the last sleep. And the geese had all the
time there is. They were the ones who remembered. They were the ones who could wait.
That was the entire strategy. It had never failed.

## V. The Young Queen Asleep

The young Queen had not slept in three nights.

I do not say this to excuse her, for there is nothing to excuse; I say it so that
you will understand that what happened was not a failure of her courage but a
limit of her flesh, and that the two are different, and that the whole of this
history turns upon the difference. Courage is infinite. A duck is not. She had
spent the three nights as her ancestress Stormbill had taught the line to spend its
nights, which is to say magnificently — going from hummock to hummock, from frightened
brood to frightened brood, quacking the cold ones warm and the lost ones home, being
everywhere, being the remembering, being the Crown. And it had worked, as it always
worked, for exactly as long as she could keep doing it.

On the fourth night, beside the southern shallows, with a duckling tucked warm
against either flank and a third complaining on her back, the young Queen of the
Duck Queendom — the seventh of her line, or the sixth, depending on a grief we have
not space for here — put her ridiculous, slipping, golden-crowned head beneath her
wing, between one quack and the next, and was asleep before she knew she was
falling.

The crown slid. It came to rest at a foolish angle against a tussock of sedge,
catching what little light the mist allowed, a small bright circle signifying
that the head beneath it was to be obeyed — and the head beneath it was fast asleep,
and could command nothing, and the whole bright pattern of the Queendom, the gather
and the rally and the answering, went out of the world as a lamp goes out, softly,
without protest, leaving forty ducks and the dark and the patient grey water of the
north.

Across the pond, in the black reeds, something shifted that had been still a very
long time.

The geese had been waiting for this exact silence for seven generations. Here, at
last, on a bad pond, on a cold night, was the thing they understood best in all the
world: a Queendom with its Queen asleep. There was no hurry. There was never any
hurry. They began, one foot at a time, to come.

## VI. The Cry

It was then that the duckling cried.

It was nobody's duckling. I want that word to fall on you with its full and dreadful
weight: *nobody's.* Its mother had been taken in the autumn — not killed, the geese
do not kill, only *moved,* harried foot by foot off the good water until she went
and did not come back, which is a thing that happens to ducks and has no song. The
duckling had been passed along after the careless manner of the flock, fed by
whoever recalled it was there, forgotten by whoever didn't, and it had ended this
night at the cold north edge of the shallows, nearest the reeds, nearest the geese,
in the worst place in the whole pond, because the worst place is where nobody's
children end.

And it woke, as the small wake, to find itself alone in the dark and the cold with
the mist coming down; and it did the one thing the small can do, which is also, let
us be honest, the one thing the great can do, in the end, when everything else is
taken from them. It cried out. A thin, high, foolish peep, with nothing in it but
the whole of its small existence, flung into the dark on the chance — on the bare,
unreasoning, infant chance — that the dark was not empty.

It was a sound the Queen would have answered. But the Queen was asleep.

Now attend, reader, for here is the hinge of the night and of the history and of
everything I have brought you this long way to see. By every law the geese had ever
proved, by the strategy that had buried six crowns, that cry should have gone out
into the mist and found nothing. The Crown was down. The remembering was asleep.
There was no white feather to make the cold one warm. The duckling cried into the
dark, and the dark, by all the patient grey arithmetic of the north, was supposed
to be empty.

The dark was not empty. At the east edge of the pond, facing the windgrass,
watching for an enemy that no one believed would come from his direction, stood a
stupid old drake named Hodge.

## VII. That Which Answered

He was not watching the duckling. He could not even see the duckling; it was on his
blind side, and far off, and he was old. He was doing the one thing he knew how to
do, the one thing a forgotten law had set in him so long ago that it had stopped
being a law and become a bone: he was standing at his post in the dark, facing the
wrong way, guarding the grass.

And he heard a small sound that was alone.

I cannot tell you what moved in Hodge then, because Hodge could not have told you,
because there were no words in Hodge of the kind that this would have required. It
was not thought; his thought was slow and usually wrong and arrived, when it
arrived at all, long after the moment had passed. It was not loyalty to the Crown;
the Crown was asleep and Hodge had half-forgotten there was one. It was not duty;
his only duty pointed east, at the grass, and the sound came from the west, from
the water, from the shallows, from the worst place, from nobody's child.

It was simply that a small sound had been alone in the dark, and Hodge had heard
it, and Hodge — old, half-blind, splay-footed, sour, the stupidest duck in the
Queendom, the joke of the east reeds, who had outlived everything he ever loved and
remembered none of it — Hodge turned from his post, and faced the water, and
answered.

It was not a beautiful sound. Let no song improve it. It was the cracked, querulous,
ill-tempered honk of an old drake who has been disturbed, flung back across the dark
water at a duckling he could not see and did not know and could not have named.
*I am here.* That is all it said. It said it badly. *I am here, and you are not
alone, and I do not know why I am telling you so, and stop that crying.*

And the duckling, hearing it, stopped crying. And was answered. And was, therefore,
no longer alone.

Now mark what followed, for this is the thing the geese had feared since before the
young Queen broke her shell, the thing they could not let take root, the thing they
had spent seven generations and the cruellest of their patience to prevent. A
second duck, woken by Hodge's ugly honk — a half-grown thing, a sibling to no one
in particular, who had no reason on earth to involve itself — heard the exchange in
the dark, the small cry and the cracked old answer, and did not go back to sleep.
It lifted its head, and it peeped, foolishly, warmly, into the night, taking up the
sound, passing it along, *I am here too,* to no command, under no crown, for no
reward, simply because the answering had reached it and the answering, once it
reaches you, asks to be passed on.

And a third voice took it up, across water the geese did not hold.

And a fourth.

It went out across the cold pond in the dark, duck to duck, the small answer of one
nobody to another, with no white feather standing over any of them to insist upon
it — the cold ones telling the colder ones *here,* the lost ones telling the more
lost ones *here,* the whole forty of them waking not to a Queen's command but to one
another's voices, until the bad pond, the shrunken last pond of the diminished
Crown, was full from end to end of the sound of ducks answering ducks for no reason
but that a sound had been alone and now was not.

The young Queen slept through all of it. That is the glory of it. She never knew. She
woke at dawn to find the night survived and could not, for the life of her, have
said how.

But the Queen was not the glory of that night. Hodge was the glory of that night,
and the nobody duckling, and the sibling who was no one's mother, and the third
voice and the fourth — and that is the whole of what I brought you here to see.

## VIII. What the Marsh Heard

Far off, on a flat grey stone in the heart of the black marsh, an old gander who had
eaten the morale of more Queens than any goose then living sat with his head turned
a quarter and held there, listening to water that was not his.

He had come to that stone, night upon night, to listen for one particular sound. He
had buried louder Queens than this seventh one; loudness did not frighten him. He
had refused braver Queens the battles they were magnificent in and let them exhaust
themselves against his patient distance; courage did not frighten him. What
frightened the old gander — the single thing in all the wide water that frightened
him — was the morning a Queen might quack, and the flock answer, and then the Queen
fall silent, as Queens must, of weariness or distance or age or death; *and the
flock go on answering one another.* For on that morning he would not have beaten a
tired voice. He would have woken a people. And a people, unlike a Queen, has no head
that a goose may make weary, and no name that a goose may scratch from a nest.

He had told his court there was one Queen, long ago, who had nearly taught the ducks
this thing, and that they had stopped her not by killing her but by poisoning the
well she dug — by stealing her credit, by standing beside her fallen banners and
honking in her own cadence, by making her people doubt the very Queen who labored to
make them need no Queen at all. Her name was a thing the geese still spoke into the
mist and the ducks no longer spoke at all. He had said: *pray that this new one is
only the seventh — loud, and brave, and ridiculous, and alone — and not the second
coming of that one.*

Now, on the cold stone, in the mist, the old gander heard a thing come across the
unheld water. It was nothing. It was a small sound, answered by a worse one — some
broken old drake's ugly honk — answered in turn by a third, and a fourth, spreading,
duck to duck, with no Queen's voice anywhere beneath it. It was nothing at all. It
was a duckling and a duckling and a fool.

The old gander listened to it for a long time. He did not move. The grey lady beside
him, who understood him better than she allowed even herself to know, looked at him
across the dark and did not make the dry sound that serves a goose for laughter, and
did not say the clever thing she had been keeping by her all evening, and something
crossed her cold and coherent heart that, in any creature but herself, she would
have called the beginning of fear.

For the well had not been poisoned this time. No one had stolen the credit, because
there was no Queen awake to take it; the night's whole salvation belonged to a stupid
old drake who would forget it by morning and to a duckling who had never known there
was anything to remember. There was nothing there for a goose to defeat. You cannot
make a people doubt their Queen when the people have just, for one night, in the
dark, with the Queen asleep, *been a people without her.*

"One foot at a time," the old gander said at last, very quietly; but he did not
believe it as he had believed it the night before, and they all heard that he did
not, and the mist came down, and on the unheld water, faintly, the answering went
on.

## IX. The Morning

Now I must tell you what this night did and did not mean, for it would be the
easiest thing in the world to tell it wrongly, and the wrong telling is the one the
geese themselves would choose for you if they could.

It would be easy to say: *the Queen was nothing, then; the crown was a fraud; throw
it in the mud; the strong stupid mass needs no leader and never did.* That is a lie,
and a dangerous one, and it is precisely the lie that has crowned the worst leaders
in every history that ever ran red. The flock did not survive that night because
leadership is worthless. The flock survived that night because of seven generations
of leadership, every one of which had poured itself out, exhausted itself, *spent*
itself, teaching ducks the one habit they finally used in the dark — and because one
Queen, long erased, had grasped the only ambition worth a crown: not to be obeyed,
but to be made, at the last, *unnecessary.* The answering that ran across the cold
pond was not the death of the Crown. It was the Crown's one true child. It was what
all that gold had been *for.*

Nor would I have you draw the opposite and gentler lie, which the ducks themselves
preferred — that one good night had saved them, that the answering would simply go
on of its own sweetness, that having found one another once in the dark they need
never trouble to find one another again. They would forget. They were ducks. By the
warm of the afternoon Hodge was back at his post facing the empty grass, and the
duckling was investigating a beetle with its whole soul, and not one bird in forty
could have told you what they had done in the night or that they had done anything
at all. The geese, who forget nothing, would come again, one foot at a time, into
exactly this forgetting. That is the duck's great flaw and the geese's great
weapon, and one night does not cure it. Mercy without memory is only a single warm
hour in a long cold history. The young Queen's true and final labor — the labor the
whole bleeding dynasty had failed at, from Hespera who gathered to Luma who
vanished — would not be to make her ducks answer one another *once,* in the dark, by
accident, with her asleep. It would be to make the answering into a thing that
*lasted;* to give the ducks' warm and forgetful mercy the geese's cold and patient
memory; to teach the flock to keep, awake and on purpose and tomorrow, the habit it
had stumbled into tonight. To wed the mercy to the memory. That marriage, and not
the taking of any marsh, is the whole of what a New Queendom would mean, and it is
the hardest thing in the world, and it is the only thing worth doing.

But that is the labor of the days that follow, and this history is the history of
one night, and the night had done its work.

So let us end where the night ended, not with the Queen, who slept, and not with
the geese, who watched, but with the least of the Crown. With an old drake, stupid
and sour and half-blind and forgotten, standing in the dark at a post whose reason
had died generations before him, facing the wrong way, guarding the empty grass —
who heard a small sound that was alone, and turned from his only duty, and answered
it badly, and in so doing did the one thing that seven crowns and a thousand banners
and the whole long golden tragedy of the dynasty had never quite managed to do: he
made the Queendom, for one night, in the dark, not need its Queen.

O reader, I told you that I would ask you to love him, and that I would not have you
laughing when the time came. The time has come. He will not be in any song. The
ducks will go on calling him the fool of the east reeds, and they will be right, and
it will not matter. For there is no act so small, performed in the dark, by one who
cannot understand it for one who will not remember it, that it does not enlarge the
world; and the marsh changed that night, and it did not change back; and somewhere
in the cold mist a patient old gander who had never in seven generations been afraid
turned his head a quarter toward the unheld water, and listened, and was.
