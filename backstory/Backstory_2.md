# A Concern of Ganders

### *An extended dialogue among the high royalty of the geese, after the manner of Count Tolstoy*

---

> *All contented marshes resemble one another; every uneasy marsh is uneasy in
> its own way.*

---

In the latter half of that cold and watchful season, when the mist lay upon the
Black Reed Marsh from the going-down of the sun until well after the herons had
risen, there gathered at the standing-water of the Gander General a society of
such geese as still mattered in the affairs of the wide water. It had been the
custom of the marsh for as long as the oldest gosling's grandmother could
misremember that the great ganders and the high grey hens should assemble when
the reeds went to seed, ostensibly to settle the boundaries of nesting and the
order of the autumn passage, but in truth, as everyone knew and no one said, to
take the measure of one another, and to discover by the angle of a neighbour's
neck whether he had grown stronger or weaker since the spring.

The Gander General held his court, if a flat grey stone and a margin of bitten
sedge may be called a court, upon the very heart of the marsh from which, the
songs said, no Duck Queen had ever returned the way she came. He was old. His
breast was the colour of a sky that has not yet decided to rain, and there was
in his eye that particular cold patience which the ducks, who forgot an injury
by sundown, had never once in seven generations managed to imitate, and which
was therefore the chief reason they had lost.

To him, on this evening, came first the Marsh Baron — heavy, deliberate, a goose
who had broken a Chorus in his youth and never afterward troubled to be charming
— and the Storm Gander, lean and restless, who could not stand upon a bank
without testing whether it would bear his weight or another's; and last, walking
upon the water as though she suspected it of belonging to someone unworthy, the
Lady Anserine, widow of a baron drowned in the Windgrass campaign, who held no
territory of her own and ruled, for that reason, a good deal of everyone
else's.

It was the Lady Anserine who spoke first, for she always spoke first, holding it
a kind of weakness to let a silence ripen that one might have curdled oneself.

"Well, mon général," she said, settling her grey skirts of feather upon the
water with the small displeased shiver of a hen who finds every seat in the
world slightly damp, "so it is true. They have crowned another one."

The Gander General did not at once reply. He was watching a place far off across
the reeds where, although nothing could be seen, something could evidently be
heard, for his head was turned a quarter and held there.

"They are always crowning one," said the Marsh Baron, who disliked the Lady
Anserine in the comfortable, lifelong manner of two creatures who have wintered
on the same water for thirty years. "It is the one thing a duck can be relied
upon to do. They cannot keep a cache, they cannot hold a bank, they cannot
remember by the evening the insult of the morning, but give them a hen with a
white feather and a loud opinion and they will put a ring of reed upon her head
and call it a Queendom. I have seen six of these crownings. I have outlived
four of the crowns."

"You have outlived four because you did nothing," said the Storm Gander, and
there was in his voice the impatience of the young, though he was not young; he
had merely never grown the patience that makes a goose seem old. "Four Queens, and
you let them die in their own time, in their own reeds, of their own age. The
Baron's strategy. Wait, and the duck will go to sleep."

"The Baron's strategy," said the Marsh Baron placidly, "is that I am here, upon
the water, with my territory beneath me, and Malloria Many-Brooded, who drove me
from the Reed Marsh and raised her pink rag upon my honk-post and held a festival
in my mud — Malloria is a coloured feather in a duck's nest that no living duck
can name. That is the Baron's strategy. I am dull, and I endure, and the
beautiful ones do not."

The Lady Anserine gave the small dry sound that in a goose serves for laughter.

"How comfortable it must be," she said, "to be dull on purpose."

---

It is necessary here to say a word concerning the geese, for those who know them
only as the ducks know them — as grey unpleasantness at the edge of the water,
as a honk in the wrong place — will not understand why this conversation
mattered, nor why it went on so long, nor why each of these four creatures
believed, with the whole of a cold and coherent heart, that it was in the right.

The geese were not wicked. This is the thing the ducks could never afford to
believe and the thing upon which, in the end, everything turned. The ducks
required their enemy to be cruel, because cruelty can be hated, and hatred is
warm, and a flock that hates together is a flock that has, for the moment,
forgotten to be hungry. But the geese were not cruel. They were *consistent*,
which is far worse, and far harder to honk at. A goose did not push a duckling
from a warm bank because he wished the duckling cold. He pushed it because the
bank, in his settled and sincere opinion, had always been meant for geese, and
the duckling was a temporary error in the arrangement of the world which he, as
a goose of conscience, was obliged to correct.

They had defeated seven Queens in this manner — or six, depending on whether one
counted the one whose name they still spoke into the mist and the ducks no
longer spoke at all — not by any single victory, but by the patient application
of a single idea across so many seasons that the idea outlived everyone who had
first believed it, and went on being true after all its believers were
coloured feathers in forgotten nests. The idea was this: *any place a goose can
hold was meant to be held by a goose.* It was not an argument. One cannot argue
with it, any more than one can argue with the tide. One can only be the duck it
is rising over.

---

"You have not asked," said the Gander General, "what I have been listening to."

He said it quietly, and the quietness did to the gathering what no loudness
could have done. The Storm Gander stilled. The Marsh Baron, who had been
arranging himself for a long and satisfying evening of being dull at the Lady
Anserine, turned his heavy head.

"I assumed," said the Lady Anserine, more gently than she had yet spoken, "that
you were listening to the new one. They say she is loud."

"They are all loud," said the General. "Hespera was loud. That is not what I am
listening to."

"Then what?"

The General was silent for a long moment, and the mist moved between them, and
far off the unseen thing made its sound again, and this time they all heard it:
not one duck, but the answer of one duck to another, and then a third, faint,
taken up across water they did not hold.

"I am listening," said the Gander General, "to whether she is alone."

---

Now the reader must be told what manner of fear this was, for it was not the fear
the Storm Gander felt, nor the contempt the Baron wore like winter down, and the
Lady Anserine, who understood the General better than she allowed even herself
to know, had gone very still upon the water.

A loud Queen did not frighten the Gander General. He had buried louder. A brave
Queen did not frighten him; Stormbill the Necessary had been the bravest thing
that ever stood crowned in the rain, and he had met her courage with the only
weapon that has ever reliably defeated courage, which is distance — he had
refused her the battle she was magnificent in, and harried the soft edges she
could not reach, and she had won every field she stood upon and lost the war
because a goose cannot be everywhere and neither, it transpired, could a Queen.

What frightened the Gander General — what had brought him to the heart of the
marsh to listen, night after night, to water that was not his — was older than
this new Queen and would outlast her if it were once permitted to take root. It
had a name. He did not say the name. The marsh said it for him sometimes, low,
when the mist came down: *Luma.*

"You are thinking of the daughter," said the Lady Anserine.

"I am thinking," said the General, "of the lesson the daughter nearly taught."

"The daughter," said the Marsh Baron heavily, "walked into this marsh and did
not walk out. There is your lesson. We honk her name to frighten goslings into
holding the line. She is a story, mon général. We made her one."

"We made her one," the General agreed, "because the alternative was that she be
true."

---

He rose then — not in anger, for the General was past anger as a stone is past
weather — and he stood upon the flat grey rock, and the three of them looked up
at him, and for a moment the old shape of the court was upon the marsh, the
shape that had outlasted six crowns.

"Listen to me," he said, "for I am old, and I have eaten the morale of more
Queens than any goose now living, and I will tell you the one thing I have
learned that is worth the telling. We have never once defeated a *duck.* Not in
seven generations. A duck cannot be defeated, because a duck does not know it is
in a war; it is looking at a beetle. What we have defeated, every time, is the
*crown* — the single mind, the one white feather, the voice that must be present
or the flock forgets it was ever a flock. We do not beat the ducks. We wait for
the Queen to be tired, or far, or dead, and then we beat the silence she leaves.

"Hespera gathered them, and we learned to scatter what is gathered.

"Bellwade organised them, and we learned to rob the keeper of his post.

"Malloria welcomed them all into one great noise, and we learned to stand at the
edges and pull the noise out of tune.

"Fenna made a peace, and we learned to keep it one foot at a time until it
strangled her.

"Plumerelle raised her banners, and we learned to knock them down in the dark
until the duckling feared the very flag that was meant to comfort it.

"Stormbill fought us, and we learned to refuse the fight.

"Every Queen solved one problem so perfectly that her solution became the wound
we opened in the next. We did not have to be clever. We only had to remember,
which ducks cannot, and to wait, which ducks will not, and to teach our goslings
what we knew, which ducks have never once managed across a single winter. That
is the whole of goose strategy. Memory, patience, and the line that moves one
foot at a time forever."

"Then we have nothing to fear from a loud child in a crown too big for her,"
said the Storm Gander.

"From the child, nothing," said the General. "From what the child might
*learn* — everything. For there was one Queen who guessed our secret. One. She
saw that the Queendom did not fail because the Queens were weak. It failed
because the Queendom *was* the Queen, and a thing that is one mind can be
outwaited by many. And so she tried — God help her, she tried — to teach the
ducks to be a Queendom when no Queen was beside them. To hold their own chorus.
To keep their own caches. To care for one another with no white feather
standing over them to insist upon it."

"And she failed," said the Baron.

"And she failed," said the General, "because it is the hardest thing in the
world, and because we did to her the cruellest thing we have ever done, which was
not to kill her but to *steal her credit* — to rob a cache and let a duck be
blamed, to stand by a fallen banner and honk in her own cadence, to make her
people doubt the one Queen who was trying to make them need no Queen at all. We
did not defeat Luma. We poisoned the well she was digging. And then her own
people, in their fear, scratched her name from the reckoning, and counted around
her, and pretended the dynasty had bent and not broken." He paused. "We remember
her name. They forgot it. That is the measure of why we have won for seven
generations. We are the ones who remember."

---

The Lady Anserine spoke now, and her voice had lost its dryness, and what was
underneath it was something none of them often heard from her, which was the
truth.

"You are afraid," she said, "that this new one will learn what Luma learned, and
that this time it will take."

"I am afraid," said the Gander General, "of the morning a Queen quacks, and the
flock answers — and then she falls silent, or goes far, or dies in her own time
in her own reeds, as Queens do — and the flock *goes on answering one another.*
On that morning the marsh changes, and it does not change back. On that morning
we have not beaten a tired voice; we have woken a people. And a people, unlike a
Queen, has no head that we may make weary, and no name that we may scratch from a
nest." He looked out at the unseen water, at the faint third voice still taking
up the call. "I came here to listen for that morning. I have been listening for
it since before she broke her shell. I should like to be very sure it is not
this one. And I find — " he stopped, and the stopping was the most frightening
thing he had done all evening — "I find I cannot be sure."

For a long time no goose spoke. The mist thickened. Somewhere a duckling, having
forgotten entirely the matter of succession because mud existed, peeped once and
was answered, foolishly, warmly, by a sibling who was not its mother, who was not
its Queen, who answered only because the small sound had been alone and now was
not.

The Gander General heard it. They all heard it. It was nothing. It was a duckling
and a duckling. It was also, conceivably, everything.

"Watch her," said the General at last, and his voice was very tired, and very
cold, and entirely without cruelty, which was the worst thing about it. "Do not
fight her where she is loud; that is what she wants, and what Stormbill taught us
to refuse. Steal her credit, not her caches. Stand beside her fallen banners.
Honk in her cadence. Make her people doubt her precisely where she is trying to
teach them not to need her. Be patient. Be consistent. Move one foot at a time.

"And pray, in whatever fashion a goose may pray, that she is only the seventh —
loud, and brave, and ridiculous, and *alone* — and not the second coming of the
one whose name we still honk into the mist because the ducks, fools that they
are, have at last had the mercy to forget it."

The Lady Anserine looked at the old goose upon the grey stone, and she did not
make the dry sound that serves a goose for laughter, and she did not say the
clever thing she had been keeping by her all evening for the purpose. She looked,
instead, toward the far water where the duckling had answered the duckling, and
something crossed her cold and coherent heart that she would have called, in any
creature but herself, the beginning of fear.

"One foot at a time," she said quietly.

And the mist came down, and covered the marsh, and on the unheld water, faintly,
the answering went on.
